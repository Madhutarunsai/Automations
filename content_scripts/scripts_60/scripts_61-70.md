## Script 61 — Teaching | Week 6, Monday
**Topic:** How to scope a project so you don't lose money
**Lead Magnet Keyword:** PRICING

**HOOK (0–3s):**
I quoted a client two days for a project. It took me two weeks. And I still got paid the same amount. Here's what I learned.

**SETUP (3–10s):**
Most freelancers guess how long something will take. Then they quote a flat price based on that guess. And when the project runs long — and it always runs long — they eat the cost. That's not bad luck. That's bad scoping.

**STORY (10–40s):**
A metal company in Texas needed their old CRM synced with their new one. I looked at it and thought — two days, maybe three. Simple data migration. Then I opened the CRM. Zero API documentation. None. I spent the first three days just figuring out how to talk to the system. I had to reverse-engineer hidden API endpoints using the browser inspector. The final workflow had 60 nodes. Ten sub-accounts. Fourteen thousand leads. Two days became two weeks. The client was happy because nobody else could crack it. But I learned the hard way — always research the API before you quote.

**LESSON (40–50s):**
Before you name a price, open the tool. Check the API docs. Test one call. If the docs don't exist, double your estimate. Then add a buffer. Scoping is research, not guessing.

Is that what's happening?" They lean forward. Now I'm not a freelancer. I'm someone who already understands their problem.

**LESSON (40–50s):**

## Script 65 — Teaching | Week 6, Wednesday
**Topic:** How to write SOPs that clients actually follow
**Lead Magnet Keyword:** SOP

**HOOK (0–3s):**
I've built automations for fifteen companies. The ones that break? They all skipped the same step. They didn't have an SOP.

**SETUP (3–10s):**
Everyone wants to automate first and document later. That's backwards. If you automate something without understanding the manual process, you'll project-creep everything. I learned this the hard way.

**STORY (10–40s):**
A staffing client came to me wanting to automate their entire hiring pipeline. Screening, scheduling, emails, the works. I asked one question: "Walk me through how you do it right now. Step by step." Silence. They couldn't. They had five recruiters and each one did it differently. No two candidates went through the same process. If I had built the automation that day, it would have broken in a week. Because you can't automate chaos. So we spent the first three days just writing down what happens. Step one — application comes in. Step two — recruiter checks the resume for these three things. Step three — if qualified, send this email. We wrote it like a recipe. Simple enough that a new hire could follow it on day one. Then — and only then — we turned each step into a node in n8n. The automation ran for six months without breaking. Because it was built on a process that actually existed.

**LESSON (40–50s):**
Manual first. Document second. Automate third. Skip any step and the system breaks. An SOP is not paperwork — it's the foundation your automation stands on.

**CTA (50–60s):**

## Script 66 — Teaching | Week 6, Wednesday
**Topic:** How to handle scope creep without losing the client
**Lead Magnet Keyword:** AUDIT

**HOOK (0–3s):**
A client once asked me to add seven new features in one week. I said yes to zero of them. They still gave me a five-star review. Here's why.

**SETUP (3–10s):**
Scope creep kills more freelance projects than bad skills. The client keeps adding "just one more thing" and you keep saying yes because you're afraid they'll leave a bad review. Then you're working sixty hours on a project you quoted for twenty. That's not being helpful. That's being a doormat.

**STORY (10–40s):**
A new staffing client kept coming to me with ideas. "I want to hire more people." "I want more clients." "I want to optimize the system." "Can we add this feature? And this one?" Every week, new priorities. I tried to keep up. After the first week I was behind on everything. So on the next call I said: "All of these are good ideas. Can I suggest something?" They said yes. I asked: "What's the one metric that matters most right now?" Silence. Then they said revenue. "How do you get revenue?" By getting clients. "Then let's do one thing. Reach out to two thousand prospects this month. Once we have responses, then we automate the rest." I framed it as phases. Phase one — leads. Phase two — features. Phase three — optimization. They agreed. We hit the lead gen goal. Then we added features. The project stayed on track because I gave them a framework, not just a "no."

**LESSON (40–50s):**
Don't say no to scope creep. Say "yes — in phase two." Give the client a roadmap so every request has a place. They feel heard and you stay on track.

Their real problem is nobody knows they exist. They don't have a lead generation problem with technology. They have a lead generation problem with effort.

**STORY (10–40s):**
This staffing firm came to me excited. They'd seen a demo of an AI voice agent that could screen candidates automatically. They wanted one built. Custom. Full integration. I asked them three questions. How many leads are you getting per month? About twenty. How many of those convert? Maybe two. How are you getting those twenty leads? Referrals mostly. I told them the truth. "You don't need a voice agent. You need two thousand prospects in your pipeline this month. Once you have five hundred responses, then the voice agent makes sense. Right now you're building a machine to sort mail — but nobody is sending you letters." They didn't love hearing it. But we built the outreach system first. Within two months their inbound tripled. Then — and only then — we built the automation to handle the volume. If we'd built the voice agent first, it would have sat there doing nothing. A toy they bought to show off. And nobody would have seen it because they weren't recognizable yet.

**LESSON (40–50s):**
If you're making under two hundred thousand a year, your only problem is lead generation. Fix that first. Everything else is a distraction dressed up as progress.
